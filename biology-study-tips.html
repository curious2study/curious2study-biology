How Do You Study Biology?

Biology rewards a different rhythm than a subject built around solving problems. Understanding sticks when you test yourself repeatedly and space that testing out over days and weeks, not when you reread your notes until they feel familiar. Familiar is not the same as remembered.

Study Tips

  1. Read the textbook before the lecture

    Walking into a lecture without having read ahead means you're hearing every term for the first time while also trying to follow the argument, and that's a hard combination. A quick read beforehand, even an imperfect one, means the lecture reinforces something you've already half-learned instead of being the first exposure. Skipping this step is one of the easiest ways to fall behind without realizing it.

  2. Test yourself with practice questions, and sort each one by how well you actually know it

    After working through a set of questions, first with your notes open, then without help once you're ready, mark each one as understood, shaky, or not yet there. Your goal for the week is to move everything out of "not yet there," not to reread the material until it feels familiar. This is the step that builds real understanding, and it's normal to get a lot wrong here at first.

  3. Give yourself a short, low-pressure pass over new material soon after you learn it

    A brief review shortly after a lecture, before the material has had time to fade, makes the next real study session far more effective than starting cold.

  4. Follow up with a focused recall session on key terms within a day or two

    Biology leans heavily on vocabulary, and a short quiz or flashcard session is where you find out what you actually remember versus what you only recognized while reading.

  5. When you review again, focus only on what you marked shaky or not yet there

    Skip what you've already marked understood. Revisiting material you've already got wastes time you could spend closing real gaps.

  6. Revisit each unit again several weeks after you've finished it, before the material has a chance to fully fade

    A short session at that point, going back over your flashcards and whatever you'd marked as weaker, is usually enough to lock a unit into long-term memory. This kind of spaced, delayed review is well-supported by cognitive science research on spacing and retrieval practice.

    Source: Carpenter, S. K., et al. "The science of effective learning with spacing and retrieval practice." Nature Reviews Psychology (2022) →

  7. Use AI as a tutor, not an answer key

    An AI chat tool can walk you through a diagram or process step by step, then quiz you on it, which is a faster way to find gaps than rereading your notes. See the AI Chat entry in Study Tools below for a full example prompt.

Study Tools

These tools map onto the habits above, each is suited to a different part of the routine, not a replacement for actually working through practice questions yourself.

NotebookLM

NotebookLM can turn your own notes, textbook chapters, or lecture slides into quizzes, flashcards, infographics, mind maps, and more. Give it a clear scope, a specific chapter, page range, or lecture slide set, rather than your whole notebook at once, so it draws from the unit you're actually studying that day.

AI Chat (Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini)

Good for working through a diagram or process out loud and getting quizzed on it in real time. Keep in mind that a chat conversation doesn't remember which concepts you marked as shaky from one day to the next, so use it to practice and get quizzed in the moment, but keep your actual tracking in NotebookLM, Anki, or Quizlet.

Example Prompt

You are my biology tutor. I'm attaching a photo of my notes or textbook page on [topic]. Please don't just summarize the material. Instead:

  1. If the topic involves a process or a diagram (like a cell cycle, a pathway, or an anatomical structure), walk me through it step by step, in the order it actually happens, rather than describing it all at once.
  2. Then stop and quiz me on it instead of re-explaining. Ask me one question at a time, mixing definitions, "what happens next" questions, and "why" questions, not just terms.
  3. After each answer, tell me if I'm right. If I'm wrong, give me a short explanation, no more than two or three sentences, and tell me whether I was close or need to revisit the concept from scratch.
  4. Then wait for me to say "next" before asking another question. Don't automatically keep explaining.
  5. Once I get three questions in a row right, move to a new question type or a related topic.
  6. Only give me a full, detailed explanation again if I ask for it directly.

AnkiWeb

ankiweb.net →

A flashcard app built around spaced repetition: it automatically schedules when you see a card again based on how well you knew it last time, so the material that keeps giving you trouble shows up more often, and the material you've mastered shows up less. Many popular courses and textbooks already have shared decks other students have built and uploaded, so it's worth searching for one before building your own from scratch.

Quizlet

quizlet.com →

A flashcard and quiz platform that's fast to set up. Create a set from your own notes, or search Quizlet's public library for one that matches your textbook or course. Its "Learn" mode is a good fit for the same-day and next-day recall sessions from the study tips above, and its matching and test modes work well as a quick check before a quiz or exam.
